WhoamI?

I have too many interests at any given time. They have evolved, devolved and convolved, But what has stayed constant is that I like dabbling in whatever looks interesting and digging deeper into some of them along the way.

I have worked as a data scientist since completing my Master of Data Science at the University of Sydney. I discovered the field after stumbling upon the R Programming course on Coursera while studying for my Bachelor's in Finance, and I later decided to pursue it as a career after getting hooked on Chris Manning's lecture "Building Neural Network Models That Can Reason", the same topic I was researching for my Master's thesis.

My pull towards AI safety came when I bumped into Rob Miles's videos on mesa-optimisers and went down the rabbit hole of finding everything I could, as I had been bored of building variations of the same ML models for some time by then. And the reason I continued on this track was the realisation that the world does not need another person to work on developing and deploying (which was really what I was doing) more and more capable AI models, so I have made it kind of a mission to work on anything that is adjacent to AI risk and safety ever since.

I am based in the sunny Eora Nation (more commonly known as Sydney), Australia, and occasionally work from Sydney AI Safety Space (SASS).

I have been generally focusing on socio-technical AI safety, which means I find myself doing a blend of both technical and policy research at any given time.

Research and Publications

2026 Incoherent Values? Probing LLM Preferences Through Parametric Variation. (Ajayi, Chowdhury, et al.) Tests whether a model’s stated preference still holds when the property the model values is made stronger or weaker and proposes a statistical framework for evaluating value coherence.

2026 Principles and Guidelines for Randomized Controlled Trials in AI Evaluation. (Kelly, Chowdhury, et al.) Proposes five principles and thirty-three guidelines for running AI human-uplift randomised controlled trials, drawing on practice from software engineering, economics, health sciences and psychology so that study results are transparent, repeatable and comparable.

2026 Mapping U.S. Federal AI Governance Against Sector Vulnerability. (Hung, Chowdhury, et al.) Compares the governance coverage of 684 U.S. federal AI governance documents across 14 sectors and 24 AI risk subdomains with how vulnerable experts rate those sectors, and finds where coverage is lacking to inform governments and industries on AI risk related decisions.

2025 An Empirical Study of Power-Seeking Behaviour in a Multi-Agent Setting. (Chowdhury) Simulates four Gemini 1.5 Flash agents running a shared art gallery for six months to test for power-seeking behavior to compare a collaborative baseline against a scenario where the agents are told to prioritise self-interest.

Passing thoughts

I care a lot about x-risks, but I take a passive role in any P(doom) discussions.

I think if we put more weight on resolving the risks we currently have than getting overly hung up on making predictions about the worst case future doom scenarios, we can naturally bring the p of the doom down quite a lot.

That is not to say we should not form ideas of how the future could look like. I only have a problem with it when it overpowers any AI risk related debate and takes the focus away from what needs to be done immediately and in near future, than what's potentially needed in fifty years time.
